1. The one thing this work fixes

The model produced an optimum. The people who fund these projects do not read optima — they read cash flows: money out, money in, year by year, cumulative, and the date it turns positive. Capex, opex, subsidies and CO2 were all inside the model, but entangled in its equations, and nothing drew them against time.

That half step now exists. The discounted cash flow equals the model's own NPV to the cent — the same object, re-presented, checked on every build. Nothing was recalibrated to make it agree.

2. Slide one — WHY

A multi-variable decision with no stable answer

Four inputs move independently, on different clocks, and the firm controls none of them. Capex falls with learning, but switching early forfeits undepreciated book value. Opex is dominated by the hydrogen price, the single largest swing factor. Subsidies are a window, not a pot — full weight to 2031, tapering to nothing by 2035. CO2 is a price that rises while free allocation phases out, and the two compound.

The honest framing: there is no single answer, and there should not be. Every plant has a different asset age, balance sheet, cost of capital and tolerance for non-productive time. What this produces is a map and a method.

Suggested for slide one. Four panels, shared year axis, each scaled to its own data — a €2.1bn spike and a €150m/yr line do not share an axis usefully. It makes the multi-variable point without a word of theory.

Two points that make this research rather than a result. The answer is a range that crosses zero — which end you believe is the whole argument, and that is uncertainty about inputs, separate from the price uncertainty the option machinery handles. Timing is far more robust than value: the corrections move the level of the answer much more than they move the year. The model is better at when than at how much.

4. The single strongest point, if only one lands

Realised volatility from the EU ETS auction record is σ = 0.31 against the 0.20 the model runs on. Sigma is the only parameter that moves the threshold without moving the break-even, so it is the only live observation that changes when. At the specification's own ‘crisis’ rung (0.30) the trigger is €140 instead of €110, and the price path crosses it in 2035 rather than 2030 — 5 more years of waiting.

The model still runs on 0.20. One year of auctions is not a price path to 2050, and nothing observed has been fed back into the parameters.

6. What must NOT be claimed

State these before anyone asks. They are the credibility of the work.

  • Opex has no time shape. The model carries one constant driven only by the hydrogen price. A real plant's operating cost rises with age, jumps at the switch while an unfamiliar technology is learned, then settles lower as contracts improve. None of that is modelled — the line is drawn flat and labelled flat rather than given a plausible curve. Open hole H10.
  • No tax layer. Electricity is 2–4× gas per kWh in every member state, and those prices exclude taxes, levies and network charges — exactly what “fiscal harmonisation” would change.
  • No MATLAB reference. The 50-case diff harness is built and its negative control works; there is no reference file to run it against.
  • Ladder rows 4–7 do not reproduce the published table. Documented case by case in CALIBRATION.md.
  • One unverified input — the €8.29/kg Platts hydrogen assessment, licensed data taken from the specification.
  • Subsidy money is not earmarked. The ETS Directive's spending clause is a non-binding target on national budgets, not an entitlement any project can draw on.
  • This is an increment, not a P&L. The cash flow is the difference between switching and not switching. Steel revenue, raw materials, debt service, tax and working capital net out by construction. A real sizing exercise starts here and adds the rest.
