Read the unit before the number

Every figure below is in one of three units, and they do not convert into each other. Confusing the first two is what made I0 = 900 look like pocket change; it is EUR 900 per tonne of annual capacity, which is EUR 2.2bn for a 2.5 Mt/yr works.

unitwhat it measureswhat it does
EUR / t capacitya stock: capex, support, the NPV itselfPer tonne of ANNUAL nameplate capacity. Multiply by annual tonnes and the denominator cancels exactly - this is the only one of the three that converts to a total.
EUR / t product / yra flow: the opex penalty, energy linesPer tonne of steel actually made, per year. Does NOT scale with plant size the way a stock does, and must never be added to one.
EUR / tCO2a price: the carbon path, the triggerPer tonne of CO2. A price, not a quantity of money the plant holds. It has no plant-size conversion at all.

The decision

Five figures on the decision itself: what the model maximises, when the threshold is reached, what the alternative costs, and how far the answer falls once the corrections are applied one at a time.

01Which year does the model actually pick, and by how much?top ↑
What am I looking at
Four lines, one per rung of the correction ladder. The horizontal axis is the commitment year T, from 2026 to the 2050 horizon. The vertical axis is NPV(T) in EUR per tonne of ANNUAL nameplate capacity - not per tonne of steel and not a total. Each line's peak is the year that rung would commit in.
Where the numbers come from
objective.npv_curve evaluated at every year in the horizon, for each of the first four rungs of scenarios.STRESS_LADDER. T* is the argmax over that curve, which is one of the model's four published outputs.
How to read it
Read across to find each peak, then read down to see what the peak is worth. The shape matters more than the level: a flat top means the year is barely determined and a year either side costs almost nothing, while a sharp peak means timing is the decision. Compare the lines vertically at a fixed year to see what one correction costs.
What it means
The peak is genuinely interior - the model is not saying 'now' or 'never', it is picking a year, which is the whole point of the exercise. The corrections move the level far more than they move the peak, so the published T* survives inputs that halve the value.
What it does NOT show
The no-investment case. NPV(T) is an INCREMENT over carrying on as before, so the do-nothing scenario is the zero line here and its size is invisible. That is exactly what the recovery chart below is for.
02When does the carbon price get high enough to justify committing?top ↑
What am I looking at
One line - the carbon price path in EUR per tonne of CO2 - against a horizontal rule at the Dixit-Pindyck trigger price. The horizontal axis is the year. The crossing is where the path meets the rule.
Where the numbers come from
The path is benefit.carbon_price_path, which interpolates the specification's published anchors (76, 86, 96, 104, 112 EUR/tCO2 for 2026-2030) rather than fitting a line through them. The rule is option.trigger_price = p*'_M x beta/(beta-1), the real-options threshold.
How to read it
Find where the line crosses the rule; that year is the crossing year, the model's fourth output. Then compare it with T* from the NPV curve: the gap between them is the option premium expressed in TIME rather than in money, and it is the single most useful number on the page for an investment committee.
What it means
The trigger sits well above the break-even price, and the distance is not a rounding error - the option multiple beta/(beta-1) is about 3.6, so waiting is worth roughly three and a half times the break-even gap. A rule that ignored irreversibility would commit far too early.
What it does NOT show
Any uncertainty about the path itself. The path is drawn as a single deterministic line because that is how the specification publishes it; the uncertainty lives in sigma, which sets the trigger's height, not in a fan around this line.
03What is the increment measured FROM, and how much of it is recovered?top ↑
What am I looking at
Two quantities on one axis in EUR per tonne of capacity: the do-nothing carbon bill, drawn as the cost it actually is rather than as a zero line, and the switch's NPV read against it. The trigger point is marked.
Where the numbers come from
regimes.recovery, which discounts the incumbent route's carbon bill over the horizon and puts the model's own NPV(T*) beside it.
How to read it
Read the do-nothing bar first - that is the exposure the plant already carries by not deciding. Then read the recovered share against it. The question the figure answers is not 'is the switch worth something' but 'how much of a bill I am paying anyway does it take back'.
What it means
This is the figure that answers the standing review comment directly. Doing nothing is not free and not neutral; it is a large discounted liability, and the switch recovers a definite share of it. Framing the decision as an increment over zero hides exactly that.
What it does NOT show
Any route other than the two compared. This is blast furnace against H2-DRI-EAF; scrap-based EAF, CCS retrofits and closure are not on the chart and are not in the model.
04How far does the case fall once the corrections are applied?top ↑
What am I looking at
One bar per rung, in the order the corrections are applied cumulatively - each rung keeps every correction above it. The axis is NPV at that rung's own T*, in EUR per tonne of capacity.
Where the numbers come from
scenarios.run_ladder over STRESS_LADDER: the unit fixes first, then the ones that are arguable, each re-solving T* rather than holding the published year fixed.
How to read it
Read top to bottom as a sequence, not as independent scenarios. The drop between two adjacent bars is what that one correction costs, given everything above it has already been applied - which is not the same as that correction's effect in isolation.
What it means
The case does not survive the corrections intact, and the fall is monotone: every correction points the same way. That directional consistency is the finding, more than any individual level.
What it does NOT show
Rows 4-7 do not reproduce the published ladder - computed NPV -49 against a published -188. CALIBRATION.md documents each disagreement and why. Read the shape, not the absolute levels of the lower rungs.
05How much does uncertainty alone move the threshold?top ↑
What am I looking at
One bar per volatility regime, ordered by sigma: cfd (0.10), long_run (0.20), crisis (0.30), realised (0.45). The axis is the trigger price in EUR per tonne of CO2.
Where the numbers come from
scenarios.sigma_ladder, re-solving the trigger at each rung of params.SIGMA_REGIMES. Sigma enters through beta, the positive root of the Bellman equation, and then through the option multiple beta/(beta-1).
How to read it
Read the spread, not any single bar. Sigma is the only input in the model that changes the trigger WITHOUT changing the break-even price, so everything you see here is pure option value - the price of keeping the decision open.
What it means
A contract-for-difference does not just subsidise; it moves the plant between rungs of this ladder by removing price variance. That is a different mechanism from a grant and it is worth more than its cash value, which is the argument this figure exists to support.
What it does NOT show
Which rung is right. The ladder is four defensible points, not a fitted sigma(t). The live figure in the market section is the first evidence in the build about where on this ladder the world actually sits.

The review comments

Four figures, one per review comment that had a table but no picture. Capex against real projects, the opex lines the model leaves out, the electricity-against-gas comparison, and the subsidy's shape.

06Is EUR 900/t of capacity a realistic number for a real project?top ↑
What am I looking at
One horizontal bar per announced European project, in EUR per tonne of ANNUAL capacity, coloured by outcome - under construction, confirmed, or cancelled. The dashed rule is the model's own I0.
Where the numbers come from
data/sourced/projects_capex.csv, five named projects with their announced total and announced capacity. The per-tonne figure is DERIVED as total divided by capacity rather than read from the CSV's own per-tonne column, which is rounded and disagrees with itself by up to 2%.
How to read it
Compare each bar against the dashed rule. Then read the outcome colour before drawing any conclusion from a bar that sits below it - the two cheap projects are a cancelled scheme and an EAF-only retrofit, which are different scopes of work rather than cheaper ways of doing the same work.
What it means
Every project still being built costs at least EUR 1,300/t of capacity against the model's EUR 900. The assumption is not absurd, but it sits at the bottom of the observed range and the model's answer is sensitive to it.
What it does NOT show
Comparable boundaries. Dunkirk is EAF only; tkH2Steel excludes the electrolyser; Boden includes 700-800 MW of one plus rolling. The bars are not like for like and the note under the chart says which is which.
07Is the operating cost actually tied to the steel industry?top ↑
What am I looking at
Cost lines in EUR per tonne of steel PRODUCT per year - a different denominator from every capex figure on this page. Lines the model's coefficient `a` actually contains are marked separately from lines it does not.
Where the numbers come from
The hydrogen and electricity lines are computed from the cached Eurostat prices and the model's own 55 kg H2 per tonne; the avoided blast-furnace costs are the specification's lumped constant. The sources are named line by line in T4.
How to read it
Read the 'in a' marks first: that is the whole of what the model's opex penalty contains. Everything marked 'NOT in a' is a real industrial cost the model does not carry. And on the hydrogen lines, pick ONE - buying hydrogen and making it on site are alternatives, not additions.
What it means
The model's opex is one lumped constant driven only by the hydrogen price. That is comment 5, and it is still open: closing it needs a line-by-line stack for both routes at one consistent boundary, which the research pass looked for and did not find.
What it does NOT show
A complete cost stack for either route. The bars shown are the lines that could be sourced, not the full cost of making steel by either method. Do not total them.
08Is electricity cheaper than gas?top ↑
What am I looking at
Paired bars for nine steel-producing member states, both in EUR per kWh so they sit on ONE axis: industrial electricity and industrial gas, excluding taxes and levies.
Where the numbers come from
Eurostat nrg_pc_205 band IE (20-70 GWh/yr) for electricity and nrg_pc_203 band I5 for gas, both excluding taxes, read from the committed cache with their period.
How to read it
Compare the two bars within each state, not across states. The ratio is the answer to the question; the level differences between countries are a separate and much noisier story.
What it means
No. Electricity runs roughly 2x to 4x gas per kWh in every member state without exception. That is why a green hydrogen floor sits above a grey one, and it is observed data rather than an assumption.
What it does NOT show
The tax layer. These prices EXCLUDE taxes, levies, network charges and the ETS indirect-cost compensation each state pays - which is precisely what 'fiscal harmonisation' would change. The model has no tax layer at all (holes H6 and H9).
09Does the SHAPE of the subsidy matter, not just its size?top ↑
What am I looking at
Bars showing what a CCfD-style per-tCO2 stream is worth once converted into the capex lump's own unit, EUR per tonne of ANNUAL capacity, at several premium levels. The dashed rule is the model's EUR 200/t lump.
Where the numbers come from
Equation (10): the premium times the abatement b = eps_inc - eps_cln, times a ten-year annuity at the model's 8% discount rate. The premium range is the mid-point band from the Commission's CCfD guidance.
How to read it
Read each bar against the dashed rule. The ratio in the label is how many times the model's lump that stream is worth - so a bar at 2.0x means the same policy delivered as a stream is worth twice what the model credits it with.
What it means
Shape is not presentation, it is value. A stream is worth 1.3x to 3.3x the lump the model nets off capex, and the German instrument runs fifteen years rather than the ten priced here, so every bar understates it.
What it does NOT show
Per-company strike prices. Those were never disclosed (hole H1), so these are the mechanism's range rather than observed awards. No bar here is a contract anyone actually signed.

The model against the market

Two figures drawn from data fetched at build time - the EU ETS auction record. Neither feeds the model. They say whether its assumptions still hold, and what the gap is worth in euros.

10Is the carbon price the model assumes anywhere near the real one?top ↑
What am I looking at
One bar per published central-path anchor, 2026 to 2030, in EUR per tonne of CO2. The dashed rule is the volume-weighted mean clearing price of the last twenty EU ETS auctions.
Where the numbers come from
The bars are benefit.CENTRAL_PATH, quoted from the v11 specification. The rule is data.eex, which reads the EEX primary-market auction report - the settlement record of the auctions EEX runs for the Member States, republished the day each one clears.
How to read it
The observation is a RULE and not a sixth bar, deliberately: it is not a replacement for the 2026 anchor. Read which anchors fall below the line and which above; the bars below it are years the specification assumed would be cheaper than the market already is.
What it means
The market is running above the 2026 anchor, which on the reference plant's 4.75 MtCO2 a year is tens of millions on the carbon bill. That makes waiting DEARER, not cheaper - it pulls the decision in.
What it does NOT show
A forecast. One year of auctions is not a price path to 2050, and nothing here is fed back into the model: Params() still carries the published anchors and a test asserts it.
11How big is the pot really, and what share would one plant take?top ↑
What am I looking at
One horizontal bar per recipient of EU ETS auction revenue this year, in euros actually received - member states and the two EU funds. The dashed rule is what the model hands a single reference plant.
Where the numbers come from
The per-auction revenue split in the EEX workbook, summed by recipient over every auction so far this year. The rule is policy.s_max = EUR 200/t capacity multiplied out at 2.5 Mt/yr.
How to read it
Read each bar against the rule to see how many plant-sized packages that recipient's receipts would fund. Then find the Innovation Fund - that is the instrument the model's s_max is actually modelled on, and it is the only bar the comparison is strictly fair against.
What it means
The pot is real and large, but the model's claim on it is not small: one works takes about a quarter of everything the Innovation Fund has collected this year, for every sector in Europe. That is a statement about priority, not just about size.
What it does NOT show
Any entitlement. None of this money is earmarked for steel - the ETS Directive's spending clause is a non-binding target on national budgets. No bar here is money this project could draw on.

The structure underneath

Five figures on how the model behaves rather than what it concludes: which input moves which output, where a coefficient changes sign, and which quantities are really the same quantity.

12If I change one input, what actually happens?top ↑
What am I looking at
A scatter. Horizontal axis: the percentage change in the trigger price from moving that lever. Vertical axis: the percentage change in NPV. Colour is the class - value only, threshold only, or both.
Where the numbers come from
findings.lever_classes, which perturbs each input in turn and records both outputs. The classification is mechanical, from the two measured changes, not assigned by hand.
How to read it
Points hugging the vertical axis move value and leave the threshold alone; points hugging the horizontal axis do the reverse. A point near the origin is a lever that does nothing. Only levers off BOTH axes trade one output against the other.
What it means
The model's inputs separate cleanly into two almost disjoint groups. That is not obvious a priori and it is useful: it means an argument about value and an argument about timing can be had independently, with different inputs.
What it does NOT show
Interactions. Each lever is moved on its own from the base case, so the map says nothing about what two simultaneous changes do. The stress ladder is the cumulative view.
13Can running the plant harder make the case WORSE?top ↑
What am I looking at
One line: the derivative of NPV with respect to the load factor u, against the hydrogen price in EUR/kg. The axis crossing at zero is the whole subject of the chart.
Where the numbers come from
findings._d_npv_du evaluated across the hydrogen price range, on the corrected configuration (kappa = 0.94, u = 0.85, real prices).
How to read it
Find where the line crosses zero - about EUR 6.15/kg. Left of it, running the plant harder helps. Right of it, the derivative is negative and running it harder hurts.
What it means
Above roughly EUR 6/kg of hydrogen, higher utilisation destroys value: the clean route's operating penalty outweighs the extra abatement, so every additional tonne makes the case worse. The sourced central hydrogen price sits near that boundary, which is uncomfortable and worth knowing.
What it does NOT show
Whether the plant can choose u. The chart treats utilisation as a dial; in practice it is set by order books and by the furnace, and the model has no capacity-constraint layer.
14Does the decision year come from economics or from the furnace?top ↑
What am I looking at
Two lines against the asset's starting age: the model's chosen commitment year T*, and the year the blast furnace is due its reline. The axis is the calendar year.
Where the numbers come from
findings.reline_capture, which re-solves the model across the whole range of starting ages and records both years at each.
How to read it
Follow the two lines from left to right. They converge, and past a certain age they coincide exactly. From that point the model is no longer choosing a year on economic grounds - it is reporting the reline date.
What it means
For an old enough asset the timing answer is mechanical, not economic. That is a boundary on where this model is informative at all, and it should be checked before quoting T* for any specific plant.
What it does NOT show
Real reline dates. The asset age is a model parameter, not a survey of the European fleet; per-plant reline calendars were not obtainable and remain an open item.
15Why does the discount rate barely move the trigger?top ↑
What am I looking at
Three lines against the discount rate, all INDEXED to 100 at the lowest rate so they share one axis: the break-even price, the option multiple, and their product, the trigger.
Where the numbers come from
findings.rate_cancellation, re-solving across r from 6% to 10%. Indexing is what lets three quantities of wildly different scale share an axis - a second y-scale would be the wrong answer.
How to read it
Watch the two input lines diverge - one rises steeply, the other falls steeply - and then watch the product line stay comparatively flat. The near-cancellation is the point.
What it means
Across the 6-10% band the break-even moves +304% and the option multiple -68%, and the trigger they multiply to moves only +29%. A sensitivity that tested the trigger alone would conclude the discount rate hardly matters, which would be badly wrong about the mechanism.
What it does NOT show
Any reason the cancellation should hold outside this band or under a different drift. It is an arithmetic coincidence of this parameterisation, not a structural law.
16Are the four reported outputs actually four different things?top ↑
What am I looking at
A rank-correlation heatmap over the model's outputs. Diverging blue to red with a neutral midpoint at zero - the cells are correlation coefficients, dimensionless, between -1 and +1.
Where the numbers come from
findings.output_correlations, Spearman rank correlations computed across the full scenario grid rather than on one path.
How to read it
Look for saturated cells away from the diagonal. A cell near +1 or -1 means those two outputs are carrying the same information, and reporting both gives a false sense of corroboration.
What it means
The four outputs are not four independent readings. Some of them move together almost perfectly, which is why the build reports T*, T_exp, the trigger and the crossing year as one answer with four faces rather than four separate results.
What it does NOT show
Causation, and behaviour outside the grid. Rank correlation over a scenario sweep says these move together here; it does not say one drives the other.
