The decision

Five figures on the decision itself: what the model maximises, when the threshold is reached, what the alternative costs, and how far the answer falls once the corrections are applied one at a time.

01Which year does the model actually pick, and by how much?top ↑
What am I looking at
Four lines, one per rung of the correction ladder. The horizontal axis is the commitment year T, from 2026 to the 2050 horizon. The vertical axis is NPV(T) in EUR per tonne of ANNUAL nameplate capacity - not per tonne of steel and not a total. Each line's peak is the year that rung would commit in.
The theory behind it
The object being maximised is not a project NPV but an INCREMENT: NPV(T) is the discounted value of switching at year T minus the value of carrying on unchanged, so the do-nothing path is the origin rather than a competitor. Each year's value is the discounted benefit stream B(t) - the carbon cost avoided by moving from 1.9 to 0.1 tCO2 per tonne, valued at the price path and net of free allocation - less the switching cost I'(T), which is capex after Wright-style learning, plus the forfeited residual book value phi*S_s(T), less any support, and less or plus the reline depending on whether the switch lands before or after it. Maximising over T is the NPV rule applied to timing rather than to a yes/no choice, and it is deliberately NOT the real-options answer: it treats the price path as known. The peak exists because two forces pull against each other - capex falls with learning and the carbon price rises, both rewarding delay, while discounting and the shrinking remaining horizon punish it. Where they balance is T*. The step at the reline year is the accounting fact that reinvestment avoided before it becomes reinvestment paid after it.
Where the numbers come from
objective.npv_curve evaluated at every year in the horizon, for each of the first four rungs of scenarios.STRESS_LADDER. T* is the argmax over that curve, which is one of the model's four published outputs.
How to read it
Read across to find each peak, then read down to see what the peak is worth. The shape matters more than the level: a flat top means the year is barely determined and a year either side costs almost nothing, while a sharp peak means timing is the decision. Compare the lines vertically at a fixed year to see what one correction costs.
What it means
The peak is genuinely interior - the model is not saying 'now' or 'never', it is picking a year, which is the whole point of the exercise. The corrections move the level far more than they move the peak, so the published T* survives inputs that halve the value.
What it does NOT show
The no-investment case. NPV(T) is an INCREMENT over carrying on as before, so the do-nothing scenario is the zero line here and its size is invisible. That is exactly what the recovery chart below is for.
02When does the carbon price get high enough to justify committing?top ↑
What am I looking at
One line - the carbon price path in EUR per tonne of CO2 - against a horizontal rule at the Dixit-Pindyck trigger price. The horizontal axis is the year. The crossing is where the path meets the rule.
The theory behind it
This is Dixit-Pindyck rather than NPV. Under irreversibility and uncertainty, committing kills an option that has value, so the correct threshold is not the break-even price p'_M but p'_M times beta/(beta-1). Beta is the positive root of the fundamental quadratic (1/2)sigma^2*beta*(beta-1) + mu*beta - r = 0, which comes out of the Bellman equation for the value of waiting when the price follows geometric Brownian motion with drift mu and volatility sigma. Beta is always greater than one, so the multiple always exceeds one, and it rises as sigma rises and falls as r rises. At the base parameters beta is about 1.386 and the multiple about 3.59 - the price must reach roughly three and a half times break-even before committing beats waiting. The economics: an irreversible commitment is the exercise of a perpetual American call whose underlying is the carbon price, and exercising at break-even throws away the premium. The crossing year is where the assumed price path first reaches that threshold, which is the real-options counterpart of T* and usually later.
Where the numbers come from
The path is benefit.carbon_price_path, which interpolates the specification's published anchors (76, 86, 96, 104, 112 EUR/tCO2 for 2026-2030) rather than fitting a line through them. The rule is option.trigger_price = p*'_M x beta/(beta-1), the real-options threshold.
How to read it
Find where the line crosses the rule; that year is the crossing year, the model's fourth output. Then compare it with T* from the NPV curve: the gap between them is the option premium expressed in TIME rather than in money, and it is the single most useful number on the page for an investment committee.
What it means
The trigger sits well above the break-even price, and the distance is not a rounding error - the option multiple beta/(beta-1) is about 3.6, so waiting is worth roughly three and a half times the break-even gap. A rule that ignored irreversibility would commit far too early.
What it does NOT show
Any uncertainty about the path itself. The path is drawn as a single deterministic line because that is how the specification publishes it; the uncertainty lives in sigma, which sets the trigger's height, not in a fan around this line.
03What is the increment measured FROM, and how much of it is recovered?top ↑
What am I looking at
Two quantities on one axis in EUR per tonne of capacity: the do-nothing carbon bill, drawn as the cost it actually is rather than as a zero line, and the switch's NPV read against it. The trigger point is marked.
The theory behind it
The accounting point the model's own framing obscures. Because NPV(T) is an increment, the do-nothing case is identically zero in every other figure - not because doing nothing is costless, but because it is the baseline the increment is measured from. That is a modelling convention, not a finding, and it systematically hides the scale of what is already being carried: an incumbent blast furnace emitting 1.9 tCO2 per tonne of steel faces a discounted carbon liability over the horizon that runs to hundreds of euros per tonne of capacity, growing as free allocation phases out under the ETS Directive and CBAM replaces it. Re-basing turns the question from 'is this investment worth making' into 'how much of a bill I am already paying does it take back', which is the form an incumbent actually faces. The recovered share is NPV(T*) over that discounted liability. It is not a rate of return and must not be read as one: the denominator is a cost avoided, not capital employed.
Where the numbers come from
regimes.recovery, which discounts the incumbent route's carbon bill over the horizon and puts the model's own NPV(T*) beside it.
How to read it
Read the do-nothing bar first - that is the exposure the plant already carries by not deciding. Then read the recovered share against it. The question the figure answers is not 'is the switch worth something' but 'how much of a bill I am paying anyway does it take back'.
What it means
This is the figure that answers the standing review comment directly. Doing nothing is not free and not neutral; it is a large discounted liability, and the switch recovers a definite share of it. Framing the decision as an increment over zero hides exactly that.
What it does NOT show
Any route other than the two compared. This is blast furnace against H2-DRI-EAF; scrap-based EAF, CCS retrofits and closure are not on the chart and are not in the model.
04How far does the case fall once the corrections are applied?top ↑
What am I looking at
One bar per rung, in the order the corrections are applied cumulatively - each rung keeps every correction above it. The axis is NPV at that rung's own T*, in EUR per tonne of capacity.
The theory behind it
A cumulative rather than a one-at-a-time sensitivity, and the difference matters. Moving each input alone and reporting the individual effects lets a reader mentally add them up, which is wrong whenever the model is non-linear - and this one is, because T* is re-solved at every rung. Each rung here keeps every correction above it and re-optimises, so the drop between two bars is that correction's marginal cost GIVEN the others, not its effect in isolation. The ordering is deliberate: the unarguable unit fixes come first (the load factor u multiplying the benefit stream only, and the hot-metal correction kappa), then the corrections that are defensible but contestable, then the sourced input ranges. Reading top to bottom is therefore reading from 'nobody disputes this' to 'this is where the argument is'. The monotone fall is itself the finding: independent corrections, each justified separately, all point the same way, which is the signature of an optimistic base case rather than of noise.
Where the numbers come from
scenarios.run_ladder over STRESS_LADDER: the unit fixes first, then the ones that are arguable, each re-solving T* rather than holding the published year fixed.
How to read it
Read top to bottom as a sequence, not as independent scenarios. The drop between two adjacent bars is what that one correction costs, given everything above it has already been applied - which is not the same as that correction's effect in isolation.
What it means
The case does not survive the corrections intact, and the fall is monotone: every correction points the same way. That directional consistency is the finding, more than any individual level.
What it does NOT show
Rows 4-7 do not reproduce the published ladder - computed NPV -49 against a published -188. CALIBRATION.md documents each disagreement and why. Read the shape, not the absolute levels of the lower rungs.
05How much does uncertainty alone move the threshold?top ↑
What am I looking at
One bar per volatility regime, ordered by sigma: cfd (0.10), long_run (0.20), crisis (0.30), realised (0.45). The axis is the trigger price in EUR per tonne of CO2.
The theory behind it
Sigma is unique among the model's inputs: it moves the trigger without moving the break-even price at all. Break-even depends on capex, the abatement, the opex penalty and the discount rate; sigma appears nowhere in it. Sigma enters only through beta, the positive root of (1/2)sigma^2*beta*(beta-1) + mu*beta - r = 0, and therefore only through the multiple beta/(beta-1). So everything this figure shows is option value in the strict sense - the price of keeping the decision open, with no change to the underlying economics of switching. Higher volatility makes waiting worth more because the payoff is convex in the price: the upside of a favourable move is captured in full while the downside is capped by not committing. That is why a contract-for-difference is worth more than its cash value. A CfD does not only transfer money; by removing price variance it moves the plant down this ladder, cutting the threshold the price must reach. The rungs are four defensible regimes, not a fitted sigma(t).
Where the numbers come from
scenarios.sigma_ladder, re-solving the trigger at each rung of params.SIGMA_REGIMES. Sigma enters through beta, the positive root of the Bellman equation, and then through the option multiple beta/(beta-1).
How to read it
Read the spread, not any single bar. Sigma is the only input in the model that changes the trigger WITHOUT changing the break-even price, so everything you see here is pure option value - the price of keeping the decision open.
What it means
A contract-for-difference does not just subsidise; it moves the plant between rungs of this ladder by removing price variance. That is a different mechanism from a grant and it is worth more than its cash value, which is the argument this figure exists to support.
What it does NOT show
Which rung is right. The ladder is four defensible points, not a fitted sigma(t). The live figure in the market section is the first evidence in the build about where on this ladder the world actually sits.

06Is EUR 900/t of capacity a realistic number for a real project?top ↑
What am I looking at
One horizontal bar per announced European project, in EUR per tonne of ANNUAL capacity, coloured by outcome - under construction, confirmed, or cancelled. The dashed rule is the model's own I0.
The theory behind it
The theory here is about boundaries, not about finance. A capex figure only means something with its scope attached, and announced project totals are drawn at wildly different boundaries: some include the electrolyser, some assume merchant hydrogen; some include rolling and finishing, some stop at the EAF; some are greenfield on serviced land, some are brownfield retrofits inheriting utilities and a grid connection. Dividing an announced total by an announced capacity gives EUR per tonne of ANNUAL capacity, which is the model's own I0 unit and makes projects of different sizes comparable - but only within a boundary. The outcome column carries the second piece of theory: survivorship. A cancelled project's announced cost is not evidence about what building one costs, because it was never built; it is evidence about what a firm was willing to claim before stopping. Treating the surviving projects as the population, and reading the cheap outliers for their scope rather than their price, is the whole method.
Where the numbers come from
data/sourced/projects_capex.csv, five named projects with their announced total and announced capacity. The per-tonne figure is DERIVED as total divided by capacity rather than read from the CSV's own per-tonne column, which is rounded and disagrees with itself by up to 2%.
How to read it
Compare each bar against the dashed rule. Then read the outcome colour before drawing any conclusion from a bar that sits below it - the two cheap projects are a cancelled scheme and an EAF-only retrofit, which are different scopes of work rather than cheaper ways of doing the same work.
What it means
Every project still being built costs at least EUR 1,300/t of capacity against the model's EUR 900. The assumption is not absurd, but it sits at the bottom of the observed range and the model's answer is sensitive to it.
What it does NOT show
Comparable boundaries. Dunkirk is EAF only; tkH2Steel excludes the electrolyser; Boden includes 700-800 MW of one plus rolling. The bars are not like for like and the note under the chart says which is which.
07Is the operating cost actually tied to the steel industry?top ↑
What am I looking at
Cost lines in EUR per tonne of steel PRODUCT per year - a different denominator from every capex figure on this page. Lines the model's coefficient `a` actually contains are marked separately from lines it does not.
The theory behind it
The model collapses the entire operating-cost difference between two production routes into one constant, a. Its theory: a = -(m_H2 * p_H2 - c_avoided), that is, hydrogen consumed per tonne at the hydrogen price, less the coke and pulverised coal the blast-furnace route no longer buys. Everything else is assumed to net out. That is a strong assumption and this figure is the audit of it. The lines marked 'NOT in a' are real, sourced industrial costs the specification does not carry on either side: DR-grade pellet premium, which is material because direct reduction needs higher-grade ore than a blast furnace; EAF electricity; graphite electrodes; scrap and alloys. The either/or on hydrogen is a physical identity rather than a modelling choice: 55 kg of hydrogen at roughly 55 kWh per kg is about 3,025 kWh per tonne of steel, which is the same energy the merchant price already pays for. Counting both the purchase and the on-site production double-counts one input.
Where the numbers come from
The hydrogen and electricity lines are computed from the cached Eurostat prices and the model's own 55 kg H2 per tonne; the avoided blast-furnace costs are the specification's lumped constant. The sources are named line by line in T4.
How to read it
Read the 'in a' marks first: that is the whole of what the model's opex penalty contains. Everything marked 'NOT in a' is a real industrial cost the model does not carry. And on the hydrogen lines, pick ONE - buying hydrogen and making it on site are alternatives, not additions.
What it means
The model's opex is one lumped constant driven only by the hydrogen price. That is comment 5, and it is still open: closing it needs a line-by-line stack for both routes at one consistent boundary, which the research pass looked for and did not find.
What it does NOT show
A complete cost stack for either route. The bars shown are the lines that could be sourced, not the full cost of making steel by either method. Do not total them.
08Is electricity cheaper than gas?top ↑
What am I looking at
Paired bars for nine steel-producing member states, both in EUR per kWh so they sit on ONE axis: industrial electricity and industrial gas, excluding taxes and levies.
The theory behind it
Two competing hydrogen production routes set two different floors, and the comparison has to be per unit of delivered energy or it means nothing. Electrolysis consumes about 55 kWh of electricity per kg of hydrogen at realistic system efficiency - hydrogen's lower heating value is 33.3 kWh/kg, so that is roughly 60% efficient. Steam methane reforming consumes about 41.1 kWh of gas per kg at 81% process efficiency, the favourable end of the reported band, and emits about 9 kg of CO2 per kg of hydrogen, which inside the ETS carries a price. So the green floor is the power price times 55 and the grey floor is the gas price times 41.1 plus the emissions at the carbon price. Both are FLOORS in the strict sense: they are energy cost alone, with no electrolyser or reformer capex, no compression, no storage and no delivery, so any real price is strictly above whichever floor binds. That is what makes a floor an argument - it cannot be argued down.
Where the numbers come from
Eurostat nrg_pc_205 band IE (20-70 GWh/yr) for electricity and nrg_pc_203 band I5 for gas, both excluding taxes, read from the committed cache with their period.
How to read it
Compare the two bars within each state, not across states. The ratio is the answer to the question; the level differences between countries are a separate and much noisier story.
What it means
No. Electricity runs roughly 2x to 4x gas per kWh in every member state without exception. That is why a green hydrogen floor sits above a grey one, and it is observed data rather than an assumption.
What it does NOT show
The tax layer. These prices EXCLUDE taxes, levies, network charges and the ETS indirect-cost compensation each state pays - which is precisely what 'fiscal harmonisation' would change. The model has no tax layer at all (holes H6 and H9).
09Does the SHAPE of the subsidy matter, not just its size?top ↑
What am I looking at
Bars showing what a CCfD-style per-tCO2 stream is worth once converted into the capex lump's own unit, EUR per tonne of ANNUAL capacity, at several premium levels. The dashed rule is the model's EUR 200/t lump.
The theory behind it
Two instruments delivering the same headline generosity are not worth the same, because a lump and a stream have different shapes in time and different risk. The model nets a one-off s_max off capex at the moment of commitment. A carbon contract-for-difference instead pays a premium per tonne of CO2 abated, every year, for a contracted tenor. Equation (10) converts the second into the first's units: the premium times the abatement b = eps_inc - eps_cln, times the annuity factor sum over k of (1+r)^-k for the tenor. At an 8% discount rate a ten-year annuity is worth about 6.7 years of payments, so a EUR 40 per tCO2 premium on 1.8 tCO2 per tonne comes to roughly EUR 480 per tonne of capacity against a EUR 200 lump. Two further asymmetries are not in the arithmetic and both favour the stream: it pays only if the plant actually abates, so it is performance-linked rather than front-loaded, and it removes carbon price variance, which by the sigma argument lowers the trigger as well as raising the value.
Where the numbers come from
Equation (10): the premium times the abatement b = eps_inc - eps_cln, times a ten-year annuity at the model's 8% discount rate. The premium range is the mid-point band from the Commission's CCfD guidance.
How to read it
Read each bar against the dashed rule. The ratio in the label is how many times the model's lump that stream is worth - so a bar at 2.0x means the same policy delivered as a stream is worth twice what the model credits it with.
What it means
Shape is not presentation, it is value. A stream is worth 1.3x to 3.3x the lump the model nets off capex, and the German instrument runs fifteen years rather than the ten priced here, so every bar understates it.
What it does NOT show
Per-company strike prices. Those were never disclosed (hole H1), so these are the mechanism's range rather than observed awards. No bar here is a contract anyone actually signed.

10Is the carbon price the model assumes anywhere near the real one?top ↑
What am I looking at
One bar per published central-path anchor, 2026 to 2030, in EUR per tonne of CO2. The dashed rule is the volume-weighted mean clearing price of the last twenty EU ETS auctions.
The theory behind it
A price path is an assumption and an auction is an observation, and the figure keeps them apart on purpose. The EU ETS is a cap-and-trade system: the cap sets supply, member states auction a share of the allowances through EEX, and the clearing price is the marginal bid that exhausts the volume on offer. That is a uniform-price sealed-bid auction, so every successful bidder pays the same clearing price and revenue divided by volume must equal it exactly - which is how this build proves it is reading the right column. A single session is noisy, so the rule is the volume-weighted mean of the last twenty, weighted because national auctions are a fraction of the size of the EU-wide ones and an unweighted mean would let a small Polish session count as much as a full EU one. The observation is drawn as a threshold, not a sixth bar, because one year of spot clearing prices is not a forecast: it says where the path starts, not where it goes.
Where the numbers come from
The bars are benefit.CENTRAL_PATH, quoted from the v11 specification. The rule is data.eex, which reads the EEX primary-market auction report - the settlement record of the auctions EEX runs for the Member States, republished the day each one clears.
How to read it
The observation is a RULE and not a sixth bar, deliberately: it is not a replacement for the 2026 anchor. Read which anchors fall below the line and which above; the bars below it are years the specification assumed would be cheaper than the market already is.
What it means
The market is running above the 2026 anchor, which on the reference plant's 4.75 MtCO2 a year is tens of millions on the carbon bill. That makes waiting DEARER, not cheaper - it pulls the decision in.
What it does NOT show
A forecast. One year of auctions is not a price path to 2050, and nothing here is fed back into the model: Params() still carries the published anchors and a test asserts it.
11How big is the pot really, and what share would one plant take?top ↑
What am I looking at
One horizontal bar per recipient of EU ETS auction revenue this year, in euros actually received - member states and the two EU funds. The dashed rule is what the model hands a single reference plant.
The theory behind it
A subsidy envelope that has been announced and money that has been received are different kinds of object, and only the second has a shape you can check. Under the ETS Directive a share of allowances is auctioned rather than allocated free; the proceeds go to member states by an allocation key, with defined slices to the Innovation Fund and the Modernisation Fund. Those funds are the instruments the model's s_max is modelled on - the Innovation Fund in particular, which awards competitively against applications rather than distributing by formula. The theory this figure tests is one of scale: the model hands a single 2.5 Mt/yr plant EUR 200 per tonne of capacity, which multiplied out is EUR 500m, and the question is whether an instrument of that size can plausibly do that for one works while remaining open to every other sector in Europe. The Directive's spending clause is a non-binding target on national budgets, not an entitlement, so no bar here is money this project could draw on.
Where the numbers come from
The per-auction revenue split in the EEX workbook, summed by recipient over every auction so far this year. The rule is policy.s_max = EUR 200/t capacity multiplied out at 2.5 Mt/yr.
How to read it
Read each bar against the rule to see how many plant-sized packages that recipient's receipts would fund. Then find the Innovation Fund - that is the instrument the model's s_max is actually modelled on, and it is the only bar the comparison is strictly fair against.
What it means
The pot is real and large, but the model's claim on it is not small: one works takes about a quarter of everything the Innovation Fund has collected this year, for every sector in Europe. That is a statement about priority, not just about size.
What it does NOT show
Any entitlement. None of this money is earmarked for steel - the ETS Directive's spending clause is a non-binding target on national budgets. No bar here is money this project could draw on.

12If I change one input, what actually happens?top ↑
What am I looking at
A scatter. Horizontal axis: the percentage change in the trigger price from moving that lever. Vertical axis: the percentage change in NPV. Colour is the class - value only, threshold only, or both.
The theory behind it
Whether two outputs are separately controllable is a structural question about the model, not a matter of taste, and it has a clean answer here. The break-even price p'_M depends on capex, the abatement, the opex flow and the discount rate. The option multiple beta/(beta-1) depends on sigma, mu and r. Only r appears in both. So an input that enters only the cost side moves value and leaves the threshold alone; an input that enters only the stochastic specification does the reverse; and r is the one genuine coupling. Plotting the measured percentage change in NPV against the measured percentage change in the trigger makes that algebra visible as geometry: near-orthogonality shows up as points hugging the axes. The classification is mechanical, read off the two measured changes rather than assigned from the algebra, which is what makes the figure a check on the implementation and not merely an illustration of it - a coding error that leaked sigma into the cost side would show as a point off both axes.
Where the numbers come from
findings.lever_classes, which perturbs each input in turn and records both outputs. The classification is mechanical, from the two measured changes, not assigned by hand.
How to read it
Points hugging the vertical axis move value and leave the threshold alone; points hugging the horizontal axis do the reverse. A point near the origin is a lever that does nothing. Only levers off BOTH axes trade one output against the other.
What it means
The model's inputs separate cleanly into two almost disjoint groups. That is not obvious a priori and it is useful: it means an argument about value and an argument about timing can be had independently, with different inputs.
What it does NOT show
Interactions. Each lever is moved on its own from the base case, so the map says nothing about what two simultaneous changes do. The stress ladder is the cumulative view.
13Can running the plant harder make the case WORSE?top ↑
What am I looking at
One line: the derivative of NPV with respect to the load factor u, against the hydrogen price in EUR/kg. The axis crossing at zero is the whole subject of the chart.
The theory behind it
The load factor u is the share of nameplate capacity actually produced, and Appendix A F2 requires it to multiply the benefit stream B(t) only, never the cost side I'(T) - capex is committed per tonne of capacity whether or not the capacity runs. Given that, the derivative of NPV with respect to u is the discounted sum of the per-tonne margin of the clean route over the incumbent. That margin has two parts of opposite sign: the carbon cost avoided, which is positive and fixed by the abatement and the price path, and the operating penalty a, which is negative and grows linearly with the hydrogen price at 55 kg per tonne. Below the crossing price the first dominates and running harder helps. Above it the second dominates, the derivative turns negative, and every additional tonne makes the case worse - the plant is paying more for hydrogen than the carbon it saves is worth. The crossing is where the two exactly cancel, and it sits uncomfortably close to the sourced central hydrogen price.
Where the numbers come from
findings._d_npv_du evaluated across the hydrogen price range, on the corrected configuration (kappa = 0.94, u = 0.85, real prices).
How to read it

14Does the decision year come from economics or from the furnace?top ↑
What am I looking at
Two lines against the asset's starting age: the model's chosen commitment year T*, and the year the blast furnace is due its reline. The axis is the calendar year.
The theory behind it
A blast furnace must be relined roughly every twenty years, and the reline is a large lumpy reinvestment. That creates a natural option date: switching instead of relining avoids the reinvestment, so committing at the reline is cheaper than committing a year after it by the whole reline cost. The model represents this as a term in the switching cost that changes sign at the reline year, which is what produces the step visible in the NPV curve. The theory question is when that lump stops being one consideration among several and becomes the entire answer. For a young asset the reline is far off, discounting flattens it, and T* is set by the balance of learning against the rising carbon price - genuine economics. For an old enough asset the reline dominates every other term and T* simply equals the reline year: the model is no longer choosing, it is reporting the maintenance calendar. Knowing which side of that boundary a specific plant sits on is a precondition for quoting T* for it at all.
Where the numbers come from
findings.reline_capture, which re-solves the model across the whole range of starting ages and records both years at each.
How to read it
Follow the two lines from left to right. They converge, and past a certain age they coincide exactly. From that point the model is no longer choosing a year on economic grounds - it is reporting the reline date.
What it means
For an old enough asset the timing answer is mechanical, not economic. That is a boundary on where this model is informative at all, and it should be checked before quoting T* for any specific plant.
What it does NOT show
Real reline dates. The asset age is a model parameter, not a survey of the European fleet; per-plant reline calendars were not obtainable and remain an open item.
15Why does the discount rate barely move the trigger?top ↑
What am I looking at

The theory behind it
The trigger is a product of two factors that respond to the discount rate in opposite directions, and the figure is about what that does to a naive sensitivity analysis. Raising r raises the break-even price, because future benefits are discounted harder while capex is paid up front - across 6-10% the break-even moves about +304%. Raising r also lowers the option multiple, because waiting costs more when capital is dearer: beta rises with r, and beta/(beta-1) falls as beta rises, giving about -68%. Their product moves only about +29%. Indexing all three to 100 at the lowest rate is what lets quantities of completely different scale share one axis; a second y-axis would be the wrong answer and would invite reading a crossing point that has no meaning. The methodological warning is general: testing a composite output alone can certify an input as unimportant while both of its components are violently sensitive, so a sensitivity analysis should test the parts, not only the product.

16Are the four reported outputs actually four different things?top ↑
What am I looking at

The theory behind it
Policy instruments are sorted here by SHAPE rather than by size, because shape is what determines value and size is what gets announced. A STREAM pays per unit of output or abatement over a contracted tenor, so its value is the payment times an annuity factor, and it is contingent on the plant actually performing. The theory behind it
Equation (10) converts a per-tCO2 stream into the capex lump's own unit so the two can be compared at all: the premium times the abatement b = eps_inc - eps_cln, times the annuity factor sum over k of (1+r)^-k across the tenor. At 8% a ten-year annuity is worth about 6.7 years of payments, so the conversion is not a small adjustment - a EUR 40 per tCO2 premium on 1.8 tCO2 per tonne of product comes to several hundred euros per tonne of capacity against a EUR 200 lump. Two asymmetries sit outside the arithmetic and both favour the stream. It pays only if the plant abates, so it is performance-linked rather than front-loaded, which is why a government can offer more of it per euro of expected cost. And it removes carbon-price variance, which by the sigma argument lowers the option value of waiting and therefore the trigger itself - an effect a lump cannot have at any size, because a lump does not touch the distribution of the price. The conversion does assume the plant abates the full b = 1.8 tCO2 per tonne for the whole tenor, which is the most favourable reading available to the stream - a ramp-up, an outage or a lower realised abatement all reduce it, and none of them touches a lump. Note also what the annuity factor is sensitive to: it is computed at the model's own 8% discount rate, so a firm with a higher cost of capital values the stream lower and the lump relatively higher, while a public body discounting at a sovereign rate values it the other way round. The same instrument is therefore worth different amounts to the two parties negotiating it, which is part of why contracts-for-difference exist at all: the state can sell certainty more cheaply than the firm can buy it.
